# Retiring a kit rule — when it is allowed, and what has to survive it

**Keywords:** rule retirement, merge, superseded rule, retired identity, ledger, non-reuse, loss proof, stated limit removal

Scope

- Included: The conditions under which an existing rule may be retired or merged into another, the reasons that never qualify, the record that proves nothing binding was lost, how a merge differs from a retirement, the ledger that keeps a retired identity permanently unusable, and what this procedure still leaves to review.
- Excluded: The mechanics of the edit — how a deletion or a move is carried out, which citations are repointed, which indexes are regenerated ([kit-rule-change-and-enforcement.md](kit-rule-change-and-enforcement.md), KIT-CHG); adding a rule and choosing its identity ([kit-rule-addition.md](kit-rule-addition.md), KIT-ADD); the structural invariants ([kit-structural-invariants.md](kit-structural-invariants.md), KIT-INV); the limits of the other kit-change steps ([kit-stated-limits.md](kit-stated-limits.md), KIT-LIM).
- Document type: Normative part — the KIT-RET family.

Binding principle: A kit whose entry procedure is written and whose exit procedure is not can only grow, and that growth is paid for on every task by every agent. But an exit without a criterion is worse than no exit: removal is the one edit that can delete an obligation while every gate stays green, because a rule that no longer exists violates nothing and reports nothing. The burden is therefore inverted here. A retirement is refused by default; it is the author who proves, clause by clause, that the obligation still binds somewhere at least as tightly — and no rule is ever shrunk, softened or dropped to buy room, comfort or speed.

## When a rule may leave

| ID | Rule |
|----|------|
| **KIT-RET-1** | A rule is retired or merged only when one of four conditions is proved, and the proof is written before the edit. (a) Superseded. The whole obligation is bound, at no lower strength, by another identity. Proof: the two texts side by side, with every clause of the leaving rule shown by name in the target. One clause missing means retirement is forbidden until that clause is first moved across intact ([**KIT-CHG-1**](kit-rule-identity-and-relocation.md)); a clause that survives only in weaker words is not superseded, it is deleted. (b) The subject is gone. The surface the rule binds no longer exists — a removed technology, a closed integration, a fence that made a stated limit false. Proof: the absence read out of the source tree, plus the released-consumer question ([**DCD-M8**](../dead-code/dead-code-rules.md)) whenever that surface is a published contract, because the oldest supported client is a consumer no scan of this tree can see. (c) A mirror, not a rule. The row restates a rule the kit does not own. This is not a retirement: the text is replaced by a named citation and the identity is kept, because it still points at an obligation whose home is elsewhere ([**KIT-LIM-7**](kit-stated-limits.md)). (d) The authority that owns the rule decides. Proof: the decision sentence recorded where that authority's decisions live. Anything outside these four is refused. |
| **KIT-RET-2** | Five reasons never qualify — each has removed a real obligation somewhere, and each reads as reasonable at the moment it is used. (a) "The file is at its size limit." A limit that costs an obligation has destroyed what it was protecting; the answer is a conceptual boundary ([**KIT-ADD-4**](kit-rule-addition.md)), never a smaller rule set. (b) "Nobody follows it." Non-compliance never invalidates a rule; it reports that the rule is unenforced, which is a fence to write, not a rule to drop. (c) "It is not mechanically fenced." Whether a rule is fenced is a routing fact, never a quality verdict ([**KIT-ENF-6**](kit-rule-change-and-enforcement.md)) — the heaviest obligations in any kit are unfenceable by definition, so a criterion built on that column removes exactly the rules review cannot replace. (d) "It is rarely needed." Frequency is not a criterion; most rules exist for rare and expensive failures, which is why they were written down instead of remembered. (e) "These two rules look alike." Similarity is not coverage — (a)'s clause-by-clause proof is the only thing that decides it, and texts that read alike routinely bind different things. |
| **KIT-RET-3** | A retirement or a merge carries a loss proof in the same change, and that proof has exactly three parts. (1) The list of prohibitions that leave. Every **MUST NOT** clause of the departing rule is enumerated one by one, and each gets either the identity that now binds it or the "subject is gone" evidence. The list may be empty, and it is still written: an empty list is a claim, and a claim can be checked, while an unwritten list cannot. (2) The direction of strictness. In a merge, name which side's scope survived — it must be the stricter side. A merge that settles on the looser wording is a regression and is refused, whatever else it tidies. (3) The hand-over of enforcement. If a check fenced the departing rule, say what happened to that check: transferred to the target identity, removed, or narrowed. A fence that vanishes with the rule it fenced leaves a dead enforcer reference behind, and scope that only ever widens is the invariant it breaks ([**FP-M18**](../false-positive/check-design-rules.md)). Where the proof is written: in the change record — the commit and the project's decision or deferred-observation register — not in the kit file. The gap sentence a retired identity leaves behind stays one sentence; turning rule files into a justification archive is how a kit's largest file becomes a migration ledger. |
| **KIT-RET-4** | A merge is not a retirement with two rules in it, and confusing them is how an obligation disappears inside tidy work. Three things bind. (a) No identity is quietly absorbed — the covered identity is retired by the full procedure ([**KIT-CHG-3**](kit-rule-identity-and-relocation.md)) and the target carries the clauses it took over byte-identically ([**KIT-CHG-1**](kit-rule-identity-and-relocation.md)); "we reworded it while merging" is a rule change hidden inside a move. (b) A target near its size limit does not license compression — if the merged text does not fit, the answer is the boundary search, not a shorter obligation. (c) The target family stays openable as a whole — if the merge spreads a family across files, every part carries the family's opening token ([**KIT-ADD-4**](kit-rule-addition.md)), or the merge has made half a family unreachable while every other gate stays green. |
| **KIT-RET-5** | A retired identity is recorded in a compact ledger, and that ledger — not any sentence inside a rule file — is the authority for what is retired. The ledger is looked up, never read: one row per identity, carrying no rule text and no history, so it can never become the archive it exists to replace. Each row holds the identity, the file whose gap sentence mirrors it, the **KIT-RET-1** condition under which it left, and — where that condition was supersession — the successor identity. It is hand-kept by necessity, and says so in its own text ([**KIT-ENF-4**](kit-rule-change-and-enforcement.md)): "this number was once used" is not a property of the current tree, and a ledger *derived* from the gap sentences would shrink the moment one of them was edited away, which is precisely the reuse it exists to stop. A ledger seeded on an existing kit records what the tree can prove and marks the rest unrecorded; it never invents a reason. A bare file-local identity is keyed by the identity and its mirror file: it is retired in that file only and stays live wherever another file defines the same string. The derivable half is kept true both ways: every ledger row's gap sentence still stands, every gap sentence's identity is in the ledger, no retired identity returns as a live definition, no kit file other than the mirror file names a retired identity, and every named successor still resolves. One direction alone is not enough — each misses the half the other catches. These are checkable, but no gate runs the check ([**KIT-LIM-12**](kit-stated-limits.md)): the author of a retirement or a new identity verifies them with the generated identity lookup, and review holds them. [**M16**](../agent-planning/agent-planning-registry-and-divergence.md) asks a work registry for the same compact ledger. This kit ships its ledger as `retired-rule-ids.tsv` at the kit root; an adopting project that retires further identities appends to that file. |
| **KIT-RET-6** | A stated limit whose premise has become false is retired, not left standing — and that is an obligation, not an option. A stated limit earns its keep by being true: it tells the next author what the tree cannot decide and which information would move the boundary. Once that information exists and the fence is built, the same sentence starts asserting that the fence is impossible — and it is read exactly where it does the most damage, by the author checking whether the question is already answered. A false limit is worse than a missing one: a missing limit leaves uncertainty, which is recoverable, while a false one produces confidence in the wrong direction. So the change that builds the fence also retires the limit under **KIT-RET-1**(b), leaves the identity gap, and names the fence in its place. What replaces it is the narrower limit the new fence still cannot decide, written as a fresh identity — never the old number back-filled. |
| **KIT-RET-7** | Stated limits of this procedure — what no check here decides, and what each would need ([**KIT-INV-8**](kit-structural-invariants.md)(b)). (a) The ledger's completeness. Five of its properties are mechanical — a retired identity never returns as a live definition, no file other than its mirror names one, both directions of the ledger-to-gap-sentence mirror hold, and every named successor still resolves — and are held by review, since no gate runs them ([**KIT-LIM-12**](kit-stated-limits.md)). Completeness is not mechanical at all. A deletion that wrote neither a ledger row nor a gap sentence leaves nothing for either half to see — the number simply looks free — and no single-checkout run finds it, because "this number once carried a rule" is not a property of the current tree; a check would need the full revision history plus a reliable statement of which revisions to compare. It is the same missing artefact [**KIT-LIM-6**](kit-stated-limits.md) records for a hand-kept list's coverage, and it is why the ledger row is written in the same change as the deletion instead of reconstructed later. (b) Whether a retirement was justified. Every condition in **KIT-RET-1** turns on a judgement no file property holds — that the target really carries every clause, that a surface is really gone, that a row really only mirrors a rule owned elsewhere. Review is the enforcement, including of whether the record of that decision stayed consistent. (c) The citing end in free prose. A planning or audit document may name a retired identity in order to record it, and no property of such a file separates that from a live citation, so those trees stay outside the citation arm by declaration. |
